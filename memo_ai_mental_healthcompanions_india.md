# Regulating AI Mental Health Companions in India

**Policy memo** | Kabir (Pratham Kapure)

## Summary

Millions of Indians now turn to AI chatbots for emotional support — often late at night, often because there's no human help anywhere near them. These products sit outside every framework we've built for mental health care. This memo argues India doesn't need a ban, and doesn't need to wait for a new statute. It needs a short list of minimum duties, scaled to risk, and enforced through the regulators we already have.

## The problem

India has a brutal gap between the people who need mental health care and the professionals available to give it. AI companions fill part of that gap because they're cheap, private, available in your language, and free of the stigma of walking into a therapist's office. That's a real benefit.

It's also the source of the risk. Someone who tells a chatbot they're thinking about self-harm is in a situation where a bad response can cause serious harm — and where the data they share is among the most sensitive things a person can produce.

I build in this space, so I see the tension from the inside. A small team can ship a useful companion quickly. But nothing in current law tells them what "good enough" actually looks like.

## What the law covers — and what it doesn't

- **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.** It requires notice, consent, and purpose limitation, and restricts children's data. But it has no category for sensitive data. A disclosure about self-harm is legally treated the same as anything else.
- **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017.** It protects the rights and confidentiality of people receiving care — but it's built around registered establishments and professionals. A companion app that never calls itself a provider falls outside it.
- **Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, 2020.** As I read them, these say AI tools may not counsel patients or prescribe. That leaves companion apps in an awkward spot: they avoid medical claims to stay outside the rules, while users treat them as a source of care.
- **Consumer Protection Act, 2019, and the IT Act.** General remedies for misleading claims and defective services, but nothing specific about crisis handling or emotional dependence.

## The gaps

1. **No clear line between a wellness product and a care product.** Companies choose their own label.
2. **Nothing requires a product to spot a crisis or hand off to a human.**
3. **Conversation data gets no extra protection** despite being some of the most sensitive data out there.
4. **Minors and people prone to leaning hard on a bot** have no tailored safeguards.
5. **No shared way to test whether these systems behave safely** — especially in Indian languages and cultural contexts, where models trained mainly on Western data tend to fall apart.

## Recommendations

1. **Regulate by function, not label.** A product that provides ongoing emotional support, or that users reasonably rely on as care, should meet minimum duties whether or not it calls itself therapy.
2. **Set baseline safety duties for all companions.** Disclose clearly that the user is talking to an AI, never claim to diagnose or treat, and escalate to human help — like the Tele-MANAS helpline — when a user signals risk of self-harm.
3. **Treat mental health conversations as high-risk data.** Separate consent before using them to train models, limit retention, ban use for advertising, and give users simple deletion rights. The Data Protection Board could issue guidance on this without amending the Act.
4. **Require pre-deployment testing and incident reporting.** Providers should test crisis handling in the languages they serve and report serious failures. Tests need to be hard to game — a system that scores well on a weak benchmark proves nothing. Independent evaluation matters more here than self-certification.
5. **Protect minors by default.** Proportionate age assurance, no engagement-maximisation features, and no personas that encourage dependence.
6. **Start with guidance and a sandbox.** The Ministry of Health and the Data Protection Board can issue binding guidance, and a regulatory sandbox can let small developers test compliance before anyone drafts a new statute.

## Objections

The strongest objection is that regulation will squeeze out small, low-cost providers and leave users with nothing. I take this seriously. The answer is to scale duties to risk and keep the baseline small enough that a two-person team can meet it.

The opposite objection — that voluntary standards are enough — ignores the fact that companies compete on engagement, and engagement and user wellbeing don't always point the same way.

## Conclusion

AI companions are already part of how Indians seek support. The legal question is no longer whether they should exist, but what they owe the people who confide in them. A short, function-based set of duties, enforced through existing bodies and tested independently, would protect users without closing the door on a technology that's helping people who have nowhere else to turn.
